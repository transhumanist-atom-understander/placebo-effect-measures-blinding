we used to believe in a placebo effect that was, like, cool.
Some objective physiological response to belief that could fight a virus or something.
By “we” I guess I mean posters on old LessWrong, though this was also prevalent in the wider culture.

People suggested using placebos as treatment.
And the idea that placebos were scientifically known to be beneficial at least in the context of trials was used to support other ideas about the power of belief, like, placebos in clinical trials shows there's at least one scientific example.

I don't think any of this holds up anymore, and I've come to believe that there's no "placebo effect" in the sense of a benefit to a patient.
Others have their own reasons for believing placebos really help people, but it's not as exciting as it used to seem: no effects on objective measures like viral infection, just on conditions like depression or pain that rely heavily on self-reports, and effects smaller than the effect of drugs.

## What's the placebo effect

In a controlled trial, the patients in the control group get better too.
In trials of depression, for example, [a score measuring the symptoms of depression decreases by about 80% as much in the placebo group as the drug group](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.0050045).

Talk of a dramatic "placebo effect" was originally inspired by such improvements.
Before-vs-after comparisons constitute almost the entirety of [Beecher's influential 1955 paper "The Powerful Placebo"](https://archive.org/download/sim_jama_1955-12-24_159_17/sim_jama_1955-12-24_159_17.pdf#page=46).

But of course the patients may have improved as much if they had simply been sent home instead of being included in the control group of a trial.
For example, [depression mostly comes in episodes measured in months](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjp.181.3.208), so we see improvements not only in depression trial control groups [but also on depression trial waitlists](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0165-0327(00)00304-9).

Truly measuring a "placebo effect" requires the unusual situation of a trial that includes both a placebo control and a no-pill control.
Such trials were collected into [a meta-analysis of the placebo effect](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20091554/) by Hróbjartsson & Gøtzsche (let's call them H&G).
I [already posted my notes on H&G](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/kpd83h5XHgWCxnv3h/why-i-don-t-believe-in-the-placebo-effect), and I'll summarize it more later.
First, a reminder of what we believed before this study.

## The placebo effect we used to believe in

The placebo effect "works not only subjectively (ie you feel less pain) but objectively (ie ulcers heal more quickly, inflammation decreases)", in [a 2009 post by Scott Alexander](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/B4AyJXYPpGbBmxQzd/what-i-tell-you-three-times-is-true).
[From Robin Hanson in 2012](https://web.archive.org/web/20230206214113/https://www.overcomingbias.com/2012/09/placebos-show-care.html), you may have heard that placebos fight cold infections by ramping up your immune system.

These were not just rationalist misunderstandings of mainstream thought.
Beecher's "Powerful Placebo" paper also claimed objective effects, and cited an effect on the common cold (where "effect", again, was just a before vs after comparison).
As for Hanson's post, after some digging [I found that the culprit was misreporting by _New Scientist_](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/kpd83h5XHgWCxnv3h/why-i-don-t-believe-in-the-placebo-effect#Example__The_common_cold).

Though we still talk about "the placebo effect" on LW, the scope and magnitude are far more limited than what we used to have in mind.
At least what we used to mean by it has been refuted.

## The placebo effect now

I don't know of any credible evidence that a placebo can fight a virus, or improves objective physiological measures.
The scope is narrowed to conditions where we rely heavily on self-reports.
The H&G meta-analysis focused especially on pain, nausea, and depression.

As for the magnitude, we can quantify it with standardized mean difference (SMD), the difference between groups relative to the variation within groups.
H&G found about 0.25 for all three conditions.

Typical drug effects for antidepressants aren't much higher than that, but those are by comparison to a placebo group.

## The placebo effect assuming placebos do nothing

And we see in the H&G meta-analysis that the placebo and no-pill groups do have some small difference in reported outcome.
No larger, I think, than we might expect for the effect of expectations on reports that justifies blinding.
Furthermore, the pattern seems right: the "placebo effect" is largest for patient-reported outcomes, whereas observer-reported outcomes had a SMD of only 0.13.

## The rhetorical role of the placebo effect

I at least don't think the placebo effect, as we now understand it, is up to the task some set it to: demonstrating the plausibility of the power of belief.
Even if we don't mean the supernatural [law of attraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_attraction_(New_Thought)), and instead just a subconscious effect on behavior, the power of positive thinking is hard to judge and subject to confirmation bias.
The placebo effect is recruited to show there's at least one scientifically confirmed example where a belief in a positive outcome brings it about.
For example, the 2009 Scott Alexander post I linked above was about Scott Adams's affirmation practice.
It's past time for the placebo effect to retire from this work.
